My philosophy of the field

Learning, design, and technology is the planned work of building the conditions under which people learn. Technology lives inside that design. It is never the design itself.

Every few years a new tool arrives and is described as the thing that will finally fix education. Every time, the tool turns out to be only as good as the instruction around it. Dale's work on learning experiences and Reiser's history of the field both point the same direction: the field matured when it moved from media to process. Moore's idea of transactional distance adds that the gap between what an instructor intends and what a learner understands is a design problem, not a geography problem.

My own work has taught me that people learn from far more than their courses. A degree audit is instruction. So is a benefits letter, a registration screen, or an onboarding checklist. When those artifacts are confusing, people make worse decisions, and no amount of good classroom teaching repairs that. I want to design those systems on purpose, starting from three questions that do not change when the tools do: who is learning, what do they need to be able to do, and how will we know it worked?

Background and education

M.Ed. in Learning, Design, and Technology

The Learning, Design, and Technology program in UNC Charlotte's Cato College of Education prepares designers to analyze learning and performance problems, design and develop instruction across delivery formats, and evaluate whether that instruction worked. Coursework blends learning theory, instructional design models, media and technology selection, and evaluation, and it ends with a capstone portfolio organized by professional standards.

I chose the Training and Development concentration because most of my career has been spent inside organizations, building tools that working adults have to learn on the job. This concentration focuses on workplace learning and human performance technology: identifying the real cause of a performance gap, deciding whether training is even the right fix, and measuring results in terms the organization cares about.

ELDT 6100: Foundations of Learning, Design, and Technology

Fall 2026, Dr. Daniel Maxwell

The first course in the program surveys the history, theories, and practice of the field. Students define the field in their own words, compare the major learning theories, examine the dispositions a professional designer needs, and propose an application project that puts theory and a design model to work on a real problem. The course also launches this portfolio.

Reflection

Writing this paper forced me to say plainly what I had only felt: the design decision matters more than the delivery device. Coming from software, I tended to start with the tool. The readings moved me toward starting with the learner and the gap I am trying to close. In hindsight, I would have checked every source against the reading list earlier instead of at the end, and I would have tested my one-sentence definition on someone outside the field before committing to it.

P2

Comparing the Three Primary Learning Theories

A three-way Venn diagram comparing behaviorism, cognitivism, and constructivism. Each theory keeps its defining traits, such as reinforcement, schema and cognitive load, and social negotiation, while the overlaps show what they share, like learning through experience, an active learner, and feedback that supports transfer.

Reflection

The hard part of this diagram was not listing traits. It was deciding where each idea actually belongs. Placing something like feedback in the center made me admit that the theories agree more in practice than their labels suggest, while things like the role of the instructor really do split them. That sorting is what later helped me pick cognitive load theory for my application project. If I did it again, I would add one example from my own work under each theory so the diagram shows how I would use it, not only what it says.

P3

Application Project Proposal: Schedule Build Coding Tutorial

A proposal for a 15-minute self-paced Articulate 360 tutorial that teaches department scheduling coordinators how to code distance education and cross-listed sections on the Registrar's course template. It includes a learner persona, an ABCD objective at the Apply level, a rationale grounded in cognitive load theory, and a development plan using the Successive Approximation Model (SAM).

Reflection

This was the first time I chose a learning theory on purpose instead of by habit. Coordinators use these codes only a few times a year, so the problem is less about memorizing and more about working memory under deadline pressure. That pointed me to chunking and worked examples. Writing the persona also kept me honest; designing for a real coordinator with 100 sections and other deadlines is different from designing for myself. In hindsight, I would tie the 4 of 5 mastery level to actual error data from past schedule builds so the target is evidence-based, not just reasonable.

Additional course work

EIST 6110: Instructional Design

This course works through a full instructional design project in stages, using a systematic design model. Part B covers the analysis that every later design decision rests on: needs, learners, context, technology, goals, tasks, and objectives.

ID-B

Building Course Sections in Banner: A Training Module for Schedule Builders

The analysis phase for a 60-minute hands-on training module in the Banner test instance. Section-build errors make up roughly 40% of the procedural error tickets the Registrar's technical team receives each term, and they reach students as wrong fees, wrong formats, and courses that count incorrectly in DegreeWorks. The analysis covers comparative, felt, expressed, anticipated, and critical incident needs, a performance gap table, learner and contextual analysis, a topic and procedural task analysis with a learning task map, and measurable objectives tied to a 50% reduction in section-build tickets within two terms.

View analysis (PDF)
Reflection

I have fixed these errors from the technical side for years, but this analysis made me ask why they keep happening. The answer was mostly a knowledge and skill gap: schedule builders copy last term's sections without knowing what each field controls. It also made me separate instructional and non-instructional fixes. Training is the main solution, but a scheduled query that flags mismatched codes catches what training misses. The biggest lesson was about evidence. Several figures in this draft are planning estimates, and before design starts I need to confirm them with a ticket report and a short survey so the 50% target is measured against a real baseline.
